# Labs copy: consolidated review and proposed revision

9 September 2026. **Status: approved revision implemented for branch preview.** The user authorized applying and pushing this copy after the consolidation pause. It supersedes the public-copy recommendations in the earlier positioning documents while preserving their research and internal capabilities.

## What was compared

I fetched and read the actual HTML served at [thirdplane.io/labs](https://www.thirdplane.io/labs/) on 9 September 2026, then compared it with the local `labs/index.html` and the separately prepared evaluation-matrix assets. The live page and local draft are different. This review does not assume the local preview is what visitors currently see.

- Live page: independent assessment of consequential AI evaluation claims, initially aimed at vendors of RL environments, training data, or evaluation systems.
- Local draft: evaluation services for AI product teams, with a shorter hero, three problems, a matrix, a compressed engagement paragraph, and a project invitation.
- Proposed copy below: retain the live page's distinctive measurement question, make the service tangible, and use the matrix as supporting explanation.

These are editorial judgments, not evidence that the revision will convert better. The broader product-team audience remains a hypothesis, supported by relevance to the work and one inbound conversation, not validated purchasing demand.

## Overall judgment

The live page's strength is its subject: what an evaluation can substantiate. It is more distinctive than the local draft's broad promise to help improve an AI product. Its weakness is that it describes an assessment instrument and its boundaries repeatedly before making the working relationship clear.

The local draft improves accessibility and contact intent, but loses too much of the original argument. The three symptoms are useful. The sentence naming an assessment, evaluation, review workflow, and next steps does not explain which of those solves which problem. The chart then occupies more space than the service offer. That imbalance should be corrected, not defended as brevity.

The proposed revision keeps the measurement question in the hero, combines each symptom with a concrete result, explains the engagement once, and assigns the chart a separate job: show why consistent scoring still needs outcome evidence. No biography, unpublished essay, invented proof, prescribed protocol, pricing, or delivery promise is added.

## Decision-by-decision comparison

### 1. Audience and category

**Live:** “independent evidence for AI measurement claims,” with an initial focus on vendors whose evaluations other organizations must rely on.

**Local:** “AI evaluation services,” implicitly aimed at teams building AI products.

**Decision:** Keep “AI evaluation services” in the masthead and explicitly name teams building AI products in the introduction. Keep training/verifier vendors as a specialist audience in internal positioning and outreach.

**Why:** The category is immediately understandable and matches the user's requested professional-services direction. Explicitly naming the audience removes an inference. This is a commercial positioning change, not merely cleaner wording; it should not be represented as already validated. The live focus is more precise for vendor assurance and should be retained if that becomes the primary business.

### 2. Hero question

**Live:** “Can your measurement system support the claim you are making?”

**Local:** “Know what to improve in your AI product.”

**Decision:** Replace the local headline with **“Does your AI evaluation measure what matters?”**

**Why:** The live question identifies the specific intellectual problem. The local headline could describe almost any AI consultancy and implies a broader diagnosis than the offer establishes. The revision preserves the relationship between measurement and intended outcome, while replacing abstract “measurement system” and formal “claim” language with words the chosen audience can recognize. It asks a useful question without guaranteeing that an engagement will settle every uncertainty.

### 3. Introduction

**Live:** A catalogue of judges, verifiers, reward signals, labels, and selection policies, packaged as dated, evidence-limited assessments.

**Local:** Find quality gaps, evaluate changes, and build reliable review workflows.

**Decision:** Use **“Thirdplane Labs provides evaluation design and review for teams building AI products.”**

**Why:** The introduction should state who the work is for and what kind of help is offered. The concrete problems and results belong immediately below, so repeating all three here is unnecessary. The specialist component list remains valuable internally but makes a poor first explanation. “Provides” describes an offer; it is not a claim of completed client results.

### 4. Problem recognition and deliverables

**Live:** A long list of decisions and counterparties, followed by a repeated assessment description.

**Local:** Three recognizable symptoms, separated from a generic list of possible outputs.

**Decision:** Combine each symptom and the relevant deliverable under **“Where I can help.”**

**Why:** This resolves the main missing information without creating two parallel lists that say the same thing. The visitor can connect a situation to work they could commission. The three entries are possible scopes, not a mandatory package.

### 5. Scores that miss failures

**Keep:** “Your scores look good, but important failures still get through.”

**Add:** An assessment of what the evaluation misses, supported by examples and priorities for correction.

**Why:** The symptom is concrete and preserves the live page's concern about scores diverging from the intended outcome. Naming the assessment and its contents makes the result tangible. It does not promise exhaustive failure detection. Detailed sampling and validation procedures remain engagement material.

### 6. Comparing changes

**Keep:** “Your team can’t reliably tell whether a change is better.”

**Add:** A working evaluation with relevant cases and criteria for comparing models, prompts, or workflows.

**Why:** “Evaluate changes” becomes an artifact the client can use. The examples identify likely decisions without limiting the offer to a specific vendor or technique. “Relevant” avoids claiming that the cases are statistically representative before the work is scoped.

### 7. Manual review

**Keep:** “Manual review is slowing development.”

**Add:** A workflow that automates suitable checks and routes uncertain cases, with supporting evidence, to human review.

**Why:** This connects the user's implementation capability to a client problem without advertising a copied bot or revealing a recipe. It describes the division of work and does not assume every judgment can be automated. It also avoids treating reviewer agreement as proof of correctness.

### 8. The live page's optimization argument

**Live:** Optimizing against a score can separate it from the outcome a customer relies on.

**Local:** Almost entirely removed.

**Decision:** Preserve the underlying warning through the chart's distinction between consistency and outcome evidence; retain the detailed optimization argument in the strategy/research documentation.

**Why:** Losing the measurement argument entirely weakened the positioning. Repeating it in a risk section, assessment section, comparison, and research teaser is unnecessary. The full training/selection argument is useful for specialist conversations but is not required to understand these services.

### 9. The objectively checkable-work exclusion

**Live:** Visitors may not need Labs if a claim is fully objectively checkable.

**Local:** Removed.

**Decision:** Keep it removed.

**Why:** It unnecessarily excludes evaluation-design and review implementation work involving direct checks. A task can contain checkable components and still need useful cases, integration, or a review process. Do not broaden this into a claim that every simple check needs a consultant; relevance can be determined during scoping.

### 10. “Without / with independent review” comparison

**Live:** Without review, there is no separate evidence path; with review, the claim has one.

**Local:** Removed in favor of a diagnostic comparison, later a directional matrix.

**Decision:** Retire the live comparison and retain the revised matrix as a conceptual explanation.

**Why:** The live contrast assumes too much about what evidence a client already has and suggests a categorical transformation produced by hiring the reviewer. The matrix describes evaluation properties without assuming the visitor is negligent. Its axes do not prove a client belongs in a particular quadrant.

### 11. Matrix direction

**Earlier local layout:** Three reviewer pairings plus a shared evidence panel, arranged as four cards without quantitative or directional axes.

**Latest local layout:** Consistency increases rightward; outcome evidence increases upward; the upper-right is the target.

**Decision:** Keep the directional layout requested by the user. Use explicit axis labels and state the coordinates within stacked mobile cards.

**Why:** A genuine two-axis matrix gives the upper-right position meaning. Reviewer pairings are not values of those axes and must not be assigned to quadrants. On phones, explicit labels preserve the relationship when physical positions disappear.

### 12. Upper-left quadrant

**Latest local:** Useful evidence, uneven judgments; reviewers apply evidence inconsistently; calibrate reviewers and clarify criteria.

**Decision:** Keep the heading, but write **“Judgments vary despite useful outcome evidence.”** Follow with **“Check how reviewers interpret the evidence and criteria.”**

**Why:** The coordinates establish variation and stronger evidence, not the cause of variation. The original body diagnoses inconsistent application prematurely. The revision asks an appropriate next question and allows differences in information, task interpretation, or legitimate priorities.

### 13. Upper-right quadrant

**Latest local:** “Dependable evaluation,” with independent outcome evidence and revalidation.

**Decision:** Keep it, while bounding the entire figure to the task and cases examined in the caption.

**Why:** This matches the requested ideal state and retains the live page's need for evidence beyond a score. It must not imply universal dependability, certification, or permanent validity. Revalidation is a useful maintenance action, not a guarantee.

### 14. Lower-left quadrant

**Latest local:** “Unclear signal,” with varying judgments and an unclear outcome link.

**Decision:** Keep it; use **“Review the criteria, evidence, and scoring process.”** as the action.

**Why:** The label fits the coordinates without asserting an unobserved failure. The action identifies what needs investigation rather than promising calibration will solve the problem.

### 15. Lower-right quadrant

**Latest local and export:** “Consistent blind spot.”

**Decision:** Reject that label. Use **“Consistent, but unvalidated.”** Body: **“Judgments agree; their connection to the intended outcome is still uncertain.”** Action: **“Test against independent outcome evidence.”**

**Why:** Weak evidence does not prove the evaluation rewards the wrong outcome or has a blind spot. That was a substantive logical error, not a style preference. The corrected label distinguishes lack of validation from demonstrated invalidity. “Unvalidated” is shorthand within this conceptual figure, not a claim that no evidence whatsoever exists.

### 16. Reviewer pairings and research sources

**Latest local:** Three diagnostic routes below the chart, plus a research disclosure.

**Decision:** Retain the user-requested AI–AI, AI–human, and human–human distinctions inside one optional **“How to read the framework”** disclosure, alongside the existing sources.

**Why:** The pairings are valuable diagnostic context, but a second visible action list repeats the chart and makes the landing page method-heavy again. “AI evaluator” is more precise than “agent” here: the diagram concerns judgments of the same work, not agents performing separate tasks. Keep the detailed research and caveats in the framework document. The cited papers inform the synthesis; none validates this particular matrix or establishes universal thresholds.

### 17. Engagement explanation

**Live:** A procedural sequence: freeze the version, define the test, compare with an independent reference, state limits.

**Local:** “Start with one product question. Get an assessment, a working evaluation or review workflow, and clear next steps—depending on what the problem needs.”

**Decision:** Replace both with an explanation of agreeing on a decision, scope, relevant examples, and deliverable, followed by handing over the work with findings, limitations, and next steps.

**Why:** The live text describes a method, not the working relationship. The local sentence bundles alternative deliverables and retreats into a qualification. The proposed services already name outputs; this section should explain how a client begins and receives the work. No fixed turnaround, price, staffing, or performance guarantee is invented.

### 18. Boundaries and proof

**Live:** Repeated disclaimers, a biography, and an unpublished essay/study teaser.

**Local:** Removed.

**Decision:** Keep biography and unpublished-work promotion off this landing page, following the user's preference. Keep limitations in the engagement description and scope in the chart caption. Do not add proof that does not exist.

**Why:** Repeated disclaimers distract from a bounded offer. The proposed language still avoids certification and universal-validity claims. Removing the biography does not eliminate the need for credibility; it leaves an explicit open question about what verifiable example the user would be comfortable sharing later. The chart illustrates a point of view, not a client result.

### 19. Contact invitation

**Live:** Twenty-minute research conversations, with language about a sales funnel and no commitment.

**Local:** “Discuss a project,” following “What are you trying to improve?”

**Decision:** Keep **“Discuss a project.”** Change the contact heading to **“What decision are you working toward?”** and ask what the visitor is building, needs to decide, and finds difficult to evaluate.

**Why:** The user wants to offer services, so a research-recruitment CTA misstates the intent. Asking about the decision elicits useful scoping information and closes the page's argument. It avoids repeating the hero's broad improvement claim. Retain the existing contact anchor and email destination; no form or scheduling promise is needed.

### 20. Metadata and social preview

**Live/social image:** Independent assessment/audits of AI training judgment.

**Local HTML metadata:** Evaluation for AI products; the old image remains referenced.

**Decision:** Use **“Thirdplane Labs — AI evaluation design and review”** as the title, a service-specific description, and **“AI evaluation design and review”** in the social image. Preserve the existing mark and cream/ink design.

**Why:** The title names the service and should match what a shared link promises. The image's older training-audit positioning contradicts the revised offer. Update the editable SVG and regenerate its PNG together when implementation resumes; changing HTML metadata alone does not fix the image.

### 21. Order, repetition, and visual scope

**Decision:** Masthead → measurement question and service category → three problem/result pairs → supporting framework → engagement → contact. Preserve the cream/ink style and shared page frame.

**Why:** Each section answers a different visitor question. The problem/result pairs do the selling; the framework explains the point of view; the engagement explains the relationship. Keep a short CTA in navigation and after the hero for convenience, then at the contact section. Functional repetition of a contact label is useful; three separate lists of the same services are not. No redesign is necessary to fix the copy.

### Consistency needs evidence

Two questions guide the work: are judgments consistent under comparable conditions, and does evidence connect them to the intended outcome?

Horizontal axis: Consistency of judgments — less consistent → more consistent.

Vertical axis: Evidence for the intended outcome — weaker → stronger.

| Position | Heading | Explanation | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Upper-left: stronger evidence, less consistency | Useful evidence, uneven judgments. | Judgments vary despite useful outcome evidence. | Check how reviewers interpret the evidence and criteria. |
| Upper-right: stronger evidence, more consistency; target state | Dependable evaluation. | Consistent judgments, supported by independent outcome evidence. | Revalidate as the system changes. |
| Lower-left: weaker evidence, less consistency | Unclear signal. | Judgments vary, and their link to the intended outcome is unclear. | Review the criteria, evidence, and scoring process. |
| Lower-right: weaker evidence, more consistency | Consistent, but unvalidated. | Judgments agree; their connection to the intended outcome is still uncertain. | Test against independent outcome evidence. |

Caption: A conceptual framework for the task and cases examined, not measured results. Agreement alone does not establish correctness; some differences in judgment are legitimate.

View the full-size diagram →

**Disclosure: How to read the framework**

Disagreement may occur between AI evaluators, between AI and human reviewers, or between people. These are places to investigate, not positions on the chart.

- AI–AI: examine variability and bias.
- AI–human: inspect mismatches and calibrate review.
- Human–human: clarify criteria and investigate disputed evidence.

The framework is Thirdplane Labs’ synthesis, informed by [Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685) on model-judge biases, [Jiang and de Marneffe](https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.78/) on human disagreement, and [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) on complementary evaluation methods.
